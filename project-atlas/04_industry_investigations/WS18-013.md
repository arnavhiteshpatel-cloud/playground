# WS18-013 — Pet waste removal weekly route (yard scooping)

> **Verdict: DROP.** Revised evidence level: **D**.
>
> The model is cheap, prepaid and simple to delegate. All three points stand.
>
> But the evidence shows a very small ticket: about $12–18 per weekly visit for one dog, or $50–75 a month [D]. Capacity is limited by yards per day, and Chicago's snow cover interrupts service every winter. No licence, density or contract builds a moat; the only barrier is the founder's own time.
>
> Year-3 operating profit: **about $25k–$115k, base about $65k.** The structural ceiling is about $10k a month. That is a fine side route but fails the mandate's "first asset of a holding company" test (G4 WARN).

## 1. Industry structure
- No Census code isolates pet waste removal. It sits in NAICS 812910 (pet care except veterinary), which has 23,686 US firms averaging $416k in receipts [C, C2-S012].
- Illinois has 963 firms in that code, 558 of them with fewer than 5 employees [C, C2-S013].
- The code also includes grooming, boarding and training, so the niche itself is **MISSING**.
- Demand rises with dog ownership. It is discretionary and cuttable in a recession (E).
- **Seasonality:** demand pauses in Chicago winters when snow covers yards (E, Stage A assumption).

## 2. Value chain and mechanism
- The homeowner pays a monthly subscription on autopay, priced by number of dogs and visit frequency.
- The technician scoops the yard, bags the waste and leaves it in the customer's trash.
- Margin comes from yards per hour, meaning density.
- Supplies cost almost nothing.
- Add-ons: deodorizing and HOA pet-station servicing.

## 3. Operator economics
- **Pricing (D, C2-S033):**
  - Typical one-dog household: $12–18 per weekly visit ($50–75 a month).
  - One New Jersey operator reports an average customer at $16 a week.
  - St. Louis guide: $15–35 per weekly visit for 1–2 dogs.
  - Tucson: $15–25.
- **Labour (C, C2-S022):** the closest wage benchmark is Chicago landscaping workers, median $45,280.
- **Franchise data:** no DoodyCalls or Scoop Soldiers Item 19 was retrieved (**MISSING**).
- **No incumbent public-company economics exist.**

## 4. Small-start evidence
- Search-surfaced anecdotes describe single operators with about 80 weekly customers at about $15 each, and a podcast titled "Earn $12K/Month Scooping Poop" [D, via search; not fetched].
- These show that **small** starts are common. They do not show that operators scale beyond one or two routes.
- **MISSING:** any documented multi-metro independent.

## 5. Competition
- **Franchises:** DoodyCalls, Scoop Soldiers.
- **Locals:** many solo operators. Entry barriers are essentially zero.
- **Switching costs:** none.
- **Platforms:** generalist gig marketplaces, which could commoditize the service (E).
- **Complaints:** missed visits, gates left open (E, not collected).

## 6. Customer purchasing behaviour
- **Who buys:** dog-owning suburban households. Decisions take days.
- **How they find a provider:** Google, Nextdoor, vets and groomers.
- **Contracts:** month to month.
- **Churn drivers:** moving, a pet's death, winter pauses, price. The rate itself is **MISSING**.
- **HOA and apartment pet stations** are a B2B variant with contracts, but small.

## 7. Failure evidence
- No bankruptcies or enforcement actions were found (**MISSING**).
- The failure mode is economic. Routes too spread out, or a founder unable to delegate, cap the business near owner-operator income. Stage A and the blind scorer both flagged this.

## 8. Regulation
- **Licensing:** no Illinois-specific licence was identified (Stage A, not verified; E).
- **Practical requirements:** local business registration and general liability insurance (E).
- **Waste disposal:** in the customer's own trash. Municipal rules are unverified.
- **Not covered by** pesticide or structural pest rules (IDOA/IDPH), unless deodorizers are registered pesticides. Some yard "sanitizers" may be EPA-registered antimicrobials, so label use would apply (E).
- **Feasibility:** effectively unrestricted.

## 9. Entry wedge test
- **The wedge:** density in dog-heavy subdivisions, reliability, and photo proof of each visit.
- **Copyability:** total, at no cost to a competitor. Nothing compounds except customer density.

## 10. Economics sanity check (year 3, before founder salary; E)

**Base case**

| Item | Annual amount |
|---|---|
| Revenue: 250 active clients × $68/month × 11.5 billed months (winter pauses) | $196k |
| Technicians: 1.5 FTE × $45,280 × 1.15 | −$78k |
| Two vehicles | −$16k |
| Marketing (replacing ~35% churn, E) | −$19k |
| Supplies | −$4k |
| Insurance | −$3k |
| Software | −$3k |
| Miscellaneous | −$8k |
| **Total costs** | **−$131k** |
| **Operating profit** | **$64k (33%)** |

Capacity check: 250 weekly yards at roughly 25–40 yards per technician-day (E) is about 1.5–2 technician-days per weekday-equivalent. That is consistent with 1.5 FTE.

**Low case:** 80–120 clients with the founder scooping. Revenue is about $57k–86k and operating profit about **$25k–32k**, all of it payment for the founder's own labour.

**High case:** 420 clients × $70 × 11.5 = $338k revenue. With 2.5 FTE and a part-time office, operating profit is about **$116k**.

**Reaching $250k a year** would need roughly 900+ clients and 5+ technicians in one metro. No source shows an independent operator at that scale.

**Why not bundle it?** Pairing it with lawn care (WS18-004) or residential pest (WS18-007) adds little. The visit cadences differ (weekly versus quarterly or seasonal), and so does the labour: unlicensed scoopers versus licensed applicators. The customer overlap is real, but a referral arrangement captures it without the operating complexity.

**Capital:** $3k–12k. ROIC is very high, but on a tiny absolute base.
