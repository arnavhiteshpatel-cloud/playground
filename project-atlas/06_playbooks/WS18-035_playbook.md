## 1. Business explanation

**What the business does.** It repairs and replaces the parts that make a garage door move, and installs new doors and openers. The business stocks parts and sends a trained technician in a van. The usual jobs:
- **Broken torsion springs** (the coiled spring above the door that lifts its weight);
- frayed lift cables, worn rollers, a door knocked off its track, failed safety sensors, a dead opener;
- every few years for a given home, a full door replacement.

**Who buys and why.** The main buyer is a homeowner whose door will not open, often with a car trapped inside. They search on a phone and call the first credible result the same day. That urgency is why the trade pays on completion with almost no receivables.

Secondary buyers:
- home sellers and buyers fixing items flagged by an inspector;
- homeowners replacing an ugly door for curb appeal;
- **light-commercial** owners: condo and townhome associations with parking-garage doors, small industrial or flex buildings, self-storage, auto shops.

Commercial owners care about uptime and safety, and some doors carry legal test duties. Rolling steel fire doors must be inspected and drop-tested at least annually by a trained technician under NFPA 80. DASMA recommends that facility managers contract a service provider for it [B, E-WS18035-S016].

**How it earns.**
- **Repairs:** a parts markup plus labour on 1–2-hour visits.
- **Openers and doors:** larger, lower-margin installs.
- **Recurring revenue** is possible only through commercial or association maintenance agreements, since residential springs last years.

Chicago-area published prices for a torsion-spring pair run **$250–400 installed**, with $100–180 of that being parts [D, E-WS18035-S009]. Premium national brands quote far more: A1 lists $350–750 for one spring [D, DF-S037], and Precision's system-wide average ticket was $1,213 in 2025 [B, C8-S019].

**Why it looked attractive:**
- non-discretionary demand tied to the housing stock;
- payment on completion;
- light licensing (no state trade licence; Home Repair Act formalities) [A, WS18-S010];
- franchise data showing single-territory revenue near $1M within three years (Access yr1 $226k → yr3 $1.02M [B, DF-S001]; Precision smallest-territory median $858k [B, C8-S019]).

The target launch area is eight suburbs (Arlington Heights, Palatine, Schaumburg, Elk Grove Village, Hoffman Estates, Mount Prospect, Rolling Meadows, Lombard). They hold about **109,000 one-unit homes**, and an 18-suburb cluster holds ~220,000 [C, E-WS18035-S014]. That is the size of Precision's smallest territory band (91k–230k single-family homes) [B, C8-S019].

**Why it is harder than it looks.** Demand is reached mostly through paid search. That channel is crowded with franchise brands, dealer showrooms and fake-listing lead generators:
- Google has sued sellers of fake Business Profiles; one named listing was "Western Los Angeles Garage Door Repair" [C, E-WS18035-S017].
- In 2025 Google removed more than 10,000 illegitimate listings run through "lead generation" call routing [C, E-WS18035-S005].
- Consumer reporting documents a $400–500 spring quote turning into a $1,258 bill [D, E-WS18035-S018].

An honest newcomer must buy the same clicks as these operators and charge less. That squeezes the margin that mature firms earn from decades of repeat and referral demand.

## 2. Commercial entry

**Initial niche.** Flat-rate residential garage door **repair** (springs, cables, rollers, off-track, openers) in the eight-suburb launch area, with replacement doors quoted on request. Alongside it, a **B2B referral layer** built from day one:
- community-association management (CAM) firms;
- home-inspection firms;
- residential brokerages.

**Light-commercial preventive maintenance** is a *year-2* line, because C.H.I.'s commercial and fire-door courses recommend 1+ years of installer experience [B, E-WS18035-S012, E-WS18035-S015]. Excluded at launch: rolling-steel fire doors, dock equipment, gates, and work inside Chicago (city licence plus electrical-licence edge cases [A, E-WS18035-S004, WS10-S006]).

**Target customer (residential).** Owner-occupants of single-family homes and townhomes built 1960–2005, with attached two-car garages on 16×7 sectional doors. Two triggers dominate: a broken spring or an inspection item. The model treats each paid job as one "customer".

**What the transactions show:**
- **Cash timing.** Residential cash arrives same day. Doors are deposit-funded. Commercial is net-30 on about 15% of revenue. The cash-conversion cycle is about two weeks.
- **Repeat potential is low.** Springs and openers last years. Repeat and referral is modelled at 4 jobs per 100 [E].
- **Failure risks:**
  - a callback on a spring job wipes out its $77 contribution;
  - a damaged door or vehicle is an insurance claim;
  - an opener sold at a "competitive" $750 loses money;
  - a paid lead that does not convert still costs $45–59 [D, E-WS18035-S006].
- **The whole business case is T1 versus T5.** The same job earns 2.4× more when it did not have to be bought.

## 4. Validation

**Minimum viable commercial offering.** One trained founder-technician with a vehicle and a starter stock of the 10 most common spring sizes, cables, rollers and sensors. On top of that: Home Repair Act-compliant insurance, a posted price menu on a Google Business Profile, a printed CAM/inspector price sheet, and a Housecall Pro account for estimates and invoices ($149–189/month Essentials [B, DF-S035]).

Doors are quoted and installed with a subcontracted experienced installer until volume justifies more.

**Customer interview plan:**
- **Homeowners (12 interviews).** Neighbours, friends and community-group volunteers who had a door repair in the last 24 months. Ask about past behaviour only: what broke, how they found the company, what they paid, what surprised them, whether they would call the same firm again.
- **B2B (15).** As in V2.
- **Inspectors (3).** How they word garage-door defects, and who buyers call.

The questionnaire is in the materials file. Nobody is contacted until the founder approves.

**Budget:**
- V0–V3: **≤ $150**.
- V4: ~$900.
- V5: **~$3,000–4,500**.
- Total before any van: **≤ ~$5,500**.

**Conditions to invest more.** V1–V6 all pass. Then buy the used van, racks and stock ($24–38k, §8). Hire the first technician only when §6's hiring rule is met.

**Conditions to abandon or pivot:**
- **Abandon:** V0 "no", or V3 fails, or both V1 and V2 fail.
- **Pivot to a B2B-led commercial door service:** V2 strongly passes but V5 fails. This needs an experienced commercial technician partner (C.H.I. commercial courses require 1+ years of experience [B, E-WS18035-S015]), so it is a different, slower business.

## 5. Operations

**Lead-to-cash workflow and classification:**

## 9. Strategic wealth creation

**As a durable cash asset.** In the base case this is a durable *job*, not a capital engine:
- year-5 operating profit of $67,046, including the founder's field labour;
- 10-year deployable surplus of $8,701;
- year-10 EV of $201,055 at 2.5×.

Mature comparables show the eventual prize only after decades of free repeat demand: one NW-suburb firm with one employee claims $171k SDE on $472k revenue [D, DF-S004].

The optimistic case ($343k year-3 operating profit; $1.49M 10-year deployable) needs three things together: Precision-average volume, a $700 ticket and $75 acquisition cost. No evidence yet shows a new independent achieving all three.

**When management could take over.** Only with ≥ $250k/yr operating profit: optimistic, or the V2/V3 variants once scaled. In base, never: delegation is unprofitable (V1).

**Can it fund other ventures?**
- Base: no.
- Conservative: it *consumes* capital.
- B2B-heavy variant: perhaps $40–80k/yr of distributable cash by years 4–5 (engine variant V2, 10-year deployable $418,440).

**Is it a strong first company for a diversified portfolio? Not as currently evidenced.**
- *For it:* essential demand, cash on completion, low licensing barriers, a real labour market for technicians, B-level revenue benchmarks.
- *Against it:* it requires the founder to become a field technician; profit rests on buying leads in a scam-polluted channel; the "honest pricing" wedge is already legally required in Chicago [A, E-WS18035-S004] and claimed by Precision; supply is dealer-gated; and conservative economics are negative.

It should rank behind finalists whose base case does not depend on the founder's manual labour. Revisit it only if V1 and V2 show premium-but-honest pricing or substantial free B2B demand.
